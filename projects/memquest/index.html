MemQuest

Interactive history with WebXR

Launch live prototype

A mobile-first learning experience that turns historical sources into entry points for scanning, augmented interpretation, spatial exploration, evidence review, and reflective practice.

The learning problem

History is easy to encounter and hard to retain.

A plaque, image, or museum label can offer facts without helping a learner connect events, people, evidence, and consequences. MemQuest asks how a phone could turn that brief encounter into an active investigation, then carry the learner's questions and progress into a deeper study experience.

My role

Designing the experience as a connected system.

My contributions spanned concept development, interaction and visual design, prototype architecture, implementation, testing, and documentation. I designed the responsive journeys, connected browser OCR and image recognition to historical content, integrated Three.js and WebXR experiences, and shaped a shared learning model for exploration and practice.

From story to inquiry

Evolving Historical Place Stories

Historical Place Stories began as a comic and an early interactive narrative about remembering the meaning of a place. Its Senso-ji prototype explored sequential scenes and choices.

MemQuest keeps the place-based curiosity but broadens the interaction model: learners identify source material, meet interpreted historical figures, inspect a reconstructed environment, compare evidence, and return for challenges. The earlier comic and prototype remain distinct artifacts in the portfolio.

Scan

One camera, two ways to recognize history.

Auto recognition coordinates local English OCR with low-resolution image search. OCR crops to the visible reading frame and compares recognized text with four configured American Revolution events. Frames and recognized text stay on the device.

For selected Stamp Act, Boston Tea Party, and Boston Massacre covers, image-target recognition can lock onto the printed source. A stable match reveals the associated event; if text and image disagree, the learner chooses rather than letting the system silently overwrite context.

Historical characters

Interpretation, clearly labeled.

A successful scan can introduce Samuel Adams for the Stamp Act, Boston Tea Party, and Continental Congress, or John Adams for the Boston Massacre and its later trials. Their 3D figures use distinct looping gestures and their profiles connect individual choices to wider political networks.

The figures and voices are artistic historical interpretations. Questions use predefined buttons and pre-generated narration clips; there is no live character intelligence, microphone recording, or synchronized lip movement.

Spatial exploration

A Boston Harbor scene built for investigation.

The procedural Three.js reconstruction turns the night of the Boston Tea Party into an explorable space. Learners can inspect four grounded clues: a hooded lantern, an East India Company tea chest, the Dartmouth, and a ship carpenter's hatchet. Drag-to-look works broadly, phone orientation adds embodied looking, and immersive WebXR is offered where supported.

Learning architecture

Beyond the moment of recognition.

A shared American Revolution learning graph connects Timeline, People, Evidence, and Memory Check inside the Library. Global Challenges adds resumable chronology, perspective, evidence, and exploration tasks. Progress keeps exploration separate from practice results, so the interface never claims a percentage of all history learned.

Privacy by scope

The learning record stays local.

Camera frames and recognized text remain on the device. Unit records, challenge drafts, practice results, and activity are stored locally in the browser. The prototype has no account system, cloud persistence, or cross-device synchronization.

Limitations

A working prototype, not a finished field system.

Recognition is limited to configured English events and selected reference covers. Image pose is not plane detection, hit testing, or a persistent world anchor. Browser support for camera, orientation, and immersive XR varies, so real Android devices, lighting conditions, print quality, motion, accessibility, thermal load, and sustained frame rate still need broader field testing.
